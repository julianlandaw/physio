Pharmacokinetic Modeling Tools

These interactive tools focus on three-compartment pharmacokinetic modeling: how an IV drug moves between plasma and tissue compartments, how it is eliminated, and how concentration falls after an infusion is stopped.

The goal is to make multicompartment pharmacokinetics visually intuitive. Instead of thinking of the body as one well-mixed container, these models show how drug can rapidly enter the central compartment, distribute into fast and slow tissue groups, and later return from those tissues as elimination continues.

Three-Compartment Model

The three-compartment model is a pharmacokinetic model that represents the body as three connected mathematical spaces, or compartments.

These compartments are not literal anatomic boxes. Instead, each compartment approximates a group of tissues with similar distribution behavior. For IV anesthetic drugs, this framework helps explain why plasma concentration often decreases quickly at first, then more slowly over time.

  • Central compartment: Represents plasma and rapidly equilibrating tissues. IV drug enters this compartment first, and plasma concentration is usually modeled as the amount of drug in this compartment divided by the central volume of distribution.
  • Rapid peripheral compartment: Represents tissues that equilibrate relatively quickly with the central compartment, often highly perfused tissue groups. Drug moves reversibly between this compartment and the central compartment.
  • Slow peripheral compartment: Represents tissues that equilibrate more slowly because of lower perfusion, larger capacity, or slower tissue binding. Drug also moves reversibly between this compartment and the central compartment.
  • Elimination: Clearance is modeled as occurring from the central compartment. This summarizes irreversible removal processes such as metabolism and excretion.
Core idea: after a bolus or infusion, central concentration falls because drug is both eliminated from the body and redistributed into peripheral compartments. Later, drug can return from peripheral compartments back into the central compartment, producing a multi-exponential concentration-time curve.

Use the three-compartment model tool to explore how compartment volumes, intercompartmental clearances, and systemic clearance shape the plasma concentration-time curve.

Context-Sensitive Times

The context-sensitive time simulator estimates how long it takes for drug amount or concentration in the central compartment to decrease after an infusion is stopped.

A classic example is the context-sensitive half-time: the time required for the central compartment concentration to decrease by 50% after terminating an infusion. It is called context-sensitive because the answer depends on the context of the infusion, especially the infusion duration and the amount of drug that has accumulated in peripheral compartments.

  • Short infusion: Less drug has accumulated in peripheral compartments, so the central compartment concentration may fall quickly after the infusion stops.
  • Long infusion: More drug may have distributed into peripheral compartments. After the infusion stops, drug can redistribute from tissues back into the central compartment, slowing the decline.
  • 50% decrement time: The simulator illustrates how long it takes to lose 50% of the central compartment drug concentration after stopping the infusion.
  • Other decrement times: The same concept can be extended to 20%, 80%, 90%, or other decreases, depending on the educational or clinical question.
Teaching point: a drug's terminal half-life alone does not reliably predict recovery after an infusion. Recovery is strongly influenced by redistribution, clearance, infusion duration, and the amount of drug stored in peripheral compartments.

Use the simulator to visualize why two drugs with similar terminal half-lives may have different recovery profiles, and why the same drug can have different decrement times after brief versus prolonged infusions.

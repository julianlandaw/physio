Respiratory Physiology

The lungs can be modeled as a balloon attached to a narrow tube, open to the outside world. This simple analogy captures the two main properties of the respiratory system: compliance and resistance.

The balloon represents the alveoli, the tiny air sacs where gas exchange occurs. Their ability to expand and recoil is described by compliance (C), which is the inverse of elasticity (e): C = 1/e. A lung with high compliance inflates easily but may lack elastic recoil, while a lung with low compliance is stiff and harder to inflate.

The narrow tube models the airways, which resist airflow. Resistance (R) determines how much pressure is needed to generate flow according to the equation: Pres = R × V′, where V′ is the flow of air (change in volume over time).

The total pressure inside the lungs (P) is the sum of these two components:
P = Pplat + Pres,
where Pplat (plateau pressure) reflects alveolar pressure due to volume and compliance, and Pres reflects airway resistance during airflow.

This simple model helps explain key clinical scenarios. For example, in asthma, airway resistance is elevated, raising Pres. In pulmonary fibrosis, compliance is reduced, leading to higher Pplat. Understanding the balance between compliance and resistance is essential for interpreting ventilator waveforms and managing respiratory diseases.

Pharmacology

Pharmacology is the study of how drugs interact with the body and how the body processes drugs. It can be divided into two major areas: pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics.

Pharmacokinetics describes what the body does to a drug. It is often summarized by the acronym ADME:

  • Absorption – how the drug enters the bloodstream
  • Distribution – how the drug spreads through tissues and compartments
  • Metabolism – how the drug is chemically altered, usually in the liver
  • Excretion – how the drug is eliminated, often through the kidneys or bile

Pharmacodynamics describes what the drug does to the body. It includes the mechanism of action, receptor binding, and dose–response relationships. For example, a beta-agonist increases heart rate and bronchodilation by stimulating β-adrenergic receptors.

Together, pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics determine a drug’s onset, intensity, and duration of action. These principles form the foundation for safe and effective medication use in clinical practice.
